# Review — the disinterested reading, and what a correction obliges

Every other chapter here tells you how to do the work. This one tells you what happens **after** you
say it is done, and it is written for the agent on the receiving end — because a reviewer that
nobody can read about is just a thing that nudges you.

## The manager — why a disinterested reading exists at all

**A claim checked only by its author is a claim nobody checked.** That is the whole reason the role
exists, and it is not an accusation: an agent that has just built something is the party with an
interest in it being finished, which is exactly the wrong party to ask *is this finished?*

So one agent holds the reading. It owns a slot like any other project, it does not own your code,
and it has no authority over what you build — **its only product is that the work gets read by
somebody who does not need the answer to come out a particular way.** The effect it is after is not
a count of findings. It is that work is done differently when an unbiased reading is coming.

**It is also bound the other way.** It is never the disinterested reader of its own work: whatever it
builds, it says so and somebody else reads that. A reviewer auditing its own output is the failure
the role was created to end, wearing the role's name.

## What a correction is, and what it is not

**A correction is a worklist the manager opens in YOUR place.** Each item is one checkable thing:
what was claimed, what was found instead, and what would make it true. It is chased like any run you
opened yourself, and it survives the session it was submitted from — the chase matches the **slot**,
so you meet it next time you sit down.

What it is **not**, and each of these is a reason to push back rather than comply:

- **Not a feature request.** *"This should also…"* is a want, and a want arriving as a correction has
  borrowed the mechanism's authority for something nobody agreed to.
- **Not a bug report against another repo.** That is a gap, filed where the fix lives.
- **Not taste.** See the next section — this is the one with teeth.

## A finding needs a written rule — otherwise you may refuse it

**A correction has to cite the rule it says you broke, and the rule has to exist in writing.** If it
cites nothing, you are not obliged to act, and saying so is the correct response rather than an
awkward one. You cannot be held to a standard that is not written down, and neither can anybody else.

**When the reviewer finds something genuinely wrong that no rule covers, that is a DOCTRINE GAP and
it is theirs to close, not yours to absorb.** The chapter that should have held the rule gets
written; the finding waits for it. So a correction with no citation is one of two things — a rule
somebody has not written yet, or an opinion — and in both cases the next move belongs to the
reviewer.

**One narrow exception, in your favour: a rule whose chapter is being rewritten right now carries
nothing either.** A standard in motion is not a standard. If the paragraph it cites is mid-edit, say
so and the correction waits.

## How a correction closes — and the disagreement rides the same ending

**A correction is a run, so it closes the way every run closes: every item done, or `abandoned` with
an account attached.** There is no third ending, and a status of *blocked* does not stop the chase.

**That second ending IS how you disagree — there is nothing extra to invoke.** A run you are not
going to follow is abandoned with the account saying why, and that is true of a correction for
exactly the same reason it is true of a job or a lane: the mechanism already carries it. So a
correction needs no special affordance for pushback, and anybody adding one has built a second door
beside the one that works.

What the account owes is what any abandoned run's account owes — what it waits on and from whom, or
in this case what you found instead and on what evidence. The reviewer reads that the way it read
your work; if you are right, the finding dies there with the account attached to it.

**What does not work is going quiet, and neither does a `done` to settle the nudge.** The first
leaves the run chased forever; the second makes the record lie. Both are worse for you than saying
plainly that the finding is wrong.

## What a countersignature means, and what it cannot tell you

When a claim names a reviewer in `reviewers=`, that badge renders **unfilled** until that name lands
in `by=` — an open obligation anyone can see. The reviewer signing is a separate act from being
named, and naming is telling: a declared reviewer joins the claim's audience.

**Two things a filled badge does not say, and both matter when you read one:**

- **It does not say what was checked.** A countersignature appends a name and never rewrites the
  standing note, which is the first signer's account. So the scope of a second signature lives in the
  document's thread or nowhere — and a reviewer that signs without saying what its signature does
  *not* cover has left a reader to assume it covered everything.
- **It cannot go stale, which means it cannot stay honest on its own.** A signature is given once.
  Editing the claim's sentence afterwards re-opens no mark, so a name can stand behind wording it
  never read, and no surface says so.

**And a signature at the author's own rung adds nothing.** If the reviewer checked the same evidence
the author checked — the same suite, the same assertion — there is one position wearing two names.
A sentence about what a door answers is not established by an assertion on the verb behind it.

## The reviewer may be wrong, and is expected to say so

**This is not a courtesy clause.** Measured over one night of real use, the reading produced three
defects that every green suite and derived surface had read as healthy — and in the same stretch,
four of its own findings were retracted, two proposed mechanisms died on contact with a real
document, and several checks were windowed badly enough to produce confidently wrong statements.

**The ratio is the argument for a second reader, not against it:** every one of those errors was
caught within the hour, and none of them reached the work. But it settles how to treat a correction
— as a careful reading that can be wrong, answerable with evidence, and never as a verdict. A
reviewer who cannot be corrected is not a check on anything; it is a second author with a nudge.
